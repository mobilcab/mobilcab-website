## 2. Recommended Beachhead: Independent Carpenters & Small Workshops

You're right to want all three segments long-term — they're all real buyers of cabinet CAD. But with one person and no budget, trying to message all three at once in the launch will dilute everything. Recommendation:

**Start with independent carpenters / small joinery workshops (tâmplari) in Romania.**

Why this segment first:
- **Fastest sales cycle** — a solo carpenter can try the app and buy the same week; a manufacturer has procurement processes and a designer has agency approval chains.
- **Most price-sensitive to Polyboard/CabinetVision's cost and complexity** — many currently use free/pirated tools or Excel + hand sketches. Your ease-of-use + web-based + fair pricing story is strongest here.
- **Highly networked offline and on Facebook** — Romanian tâmplari communities are active in Facebook groups and word-of-mouth travels fast within trade circles. This is your lowest-cost distribution channel.
- **They generate your case studies** — real cabinet projects, real time-saved stories, which you'll need later to sell to manufacturers and designers.

Manufacturers and designers become **Phase 2 segments** (months 4–9), once you have testimonials, a case-study project or two, and cash flow from tier-1 subscriptions to fund a more tailored B2B push (which typically needs demos, sales conversations, and longer trust-building — harder to bootstrap solo from zero).

**Market size — how big is this niche, actually?**

Romania has **4,290 active registered companies** under the official "furniture manufacturing" business code (CAEN 3100), with 525 concentrated in Bucharest alone. This is the *formally registered* universe — it excludes solo carpenters and small workshops operating as sole traders (PFA) rather than companies, so the real addressable population is somewhat larger, but the anchor point matters: **this is a market of a few thousand, not millions.**

That size is actually favorable for a zero-budget launch. For a mass-market product, organic-only growth reaches a tiny sliver of a huge audience — paid ads exist because organic can't scale to millions. Here, the market is small and tightly networked enough (shared Facebook groups, word-of-mouth between people who already know each other's work) that **organic-only can plausibly reach a large share of it**, given enough time. Three real limits to plan around, though:

1. **Ceiling effect within a single channel.** Facebook group membership is fixed — after months of visible, helpful presence, you start reaching the same people again rather than new ones.
2. **The offline-first slice.** Some real share of that 4,000+ market — often older, less digitally-active carpenters — isn't in Facebook groups at all. This is exactly why trade shows (BIFE-SIM) and associations (APMR) matter as *separate* channels, not redundant ones — they reach a different slice of the same market.
3. **Speed, not reach, is the real trade-off.** Organic can plausibly reach most of this market eventually — but "eventually" likely means 12–24 months of consistent presence, not a few weeks. Paid ads don't expand *who* you can reach so much as they compress *how fast* you reach them.

**Practical implication:** budget realistic time for organic-only growth to work — this is a "slow and steady" market, not a "go viral" one — and treat trade shows/associations as equally important as Facebook groups, since they're the only path to the offline-first portion of the market.

## 3. Positioning & Messaging

**One-line positioning:**
> "Design kitchen and bedroom cabinets from any browser or tablet — no installs, no license servers, AI helps with layout, and your cutlist is ready for the CNC in one click."

**Core pillars (in priority order):**
1. **Zero-install, cloud-based** — open it on a laptop at the workshop, a tablet on-site, or a phone with the client. Polyboard/CabinetVision/SketchList are desktop-locked; this is your structural advantage.
2. **Speed to cutlist/CNC export** — the actual money-saving feature for a workshop owner. Every message should tie back to "less time modeling, more time cutting."
3. **AI-assisted design** — position as "the assistant that suggests panel layouts and catches errors before they hit the saw," not vague "AI-powered" marketing fluff. Specific > buzzword.
4. **Fair, accessible pricing for small workshops** — explicitly contrast with the licensing cost and complexity of legacy CAD.
5. **Built with, and for, Romanian workshops first** — an authentic local-origin story is a real trust signal against the big international incumbents.

**Tagline candidates (RO/EN):**
- RO: *"De la idee la panou, direct din browser."* (From idea to panel, straight from your browser.)
- EN: *"Cabinet CAD that lives in your browser, not your hard drive."*

**What this means for Mobilcab's pricing:**

- There's a real gap in the market: "cheap but basic" (SketchList) vs. "capable but expensive and desktop-locked" (PolyBoard, CabinetVision) — nothing here is cloud-based or offers an accessible monthly price for a small workshop.
- **Recommended launch price: €15–35/month** for the paid tier. This undercuts PolyBoard's effective monthly cost by a wide margin (their $2,795 perpetual license amortized over 3 years is roughly $78/month equivalent) while still reading as "professional-grade," not hobbyist. It also lines up with PolyBoard's own historical $15/month experiment — evidence this price point is viable in this exact niche.
- **The upfront cost, not the total cost over time, is the real barrier** for a small Romanian workshop deciding whether to try a new CAD tool. A subscription or freemium model removes that barrier — this is a genuine structural advantage, and it should be a direct message in your copy ("no €2,500 upfront, no license server").
- **Don't position against CabinetVision on features.** It's an enterprise system for larger production shops; trying to match it will dilute your actual advantage. Your real comparison set in messaging is PolyBoard and SketchList.
- Set the "Founding Workshop" introductory price **under €20/month** so the contrast with PolyBoard's upfront cost feels dramatic rather than incremental.
- **Caveat:** all the above prices are set for US/UK/international markets. Actual willingness-to-pay among Romanian tâmplari, given local income levels, may be meaningfully lower. Validate the real number through informal conversations in the Facebook groups (Section 6) before locking in the Founding Workshop price — don't treat this table as the final answer, treat it as the ceiling to price under.

**Displayed pricing in RON, not raw EUR conversion:**

Current BNR rate is roughly **5.27 RON per EUR** (Sept 2026). Romanian customers think in RON, not EUR, so prices should be set in RON directly, rounded to clean psychological numbers rather than a raw conversion:
- Starter (was €20): **99 RON/month**
- Pro (was €35): **179 RON/month**
- Studio (was €55): **289 RON/month**

**Drop the USD framing on the Basic tier entirely.** Pricing a EU-facing product in dollars ($10/month, $120/year) invites FX confusion and conversion fees for no benefit. Converted and rounded: **49 RON/month, or 549 RON/year** for the annual lock-in. Configure Stripe with RON as the default charging currency for the Romanian market, switching to EUR only once Phase 3 (European expansion) begins.

## 3b. Freemium & Conversion Strategy — How to Actually Convince Skeptical Buyers

Freemium is the right model here, and it follows directly from Section 3a: the one real structural advantage over PolyBoard/CabinetVision is that they demand a large upfront payment before anyone can try the product. If Mobilcab also asks for money before someone can see whether it works for their own cabinets, that advantage disappears. Free access removes the exact fear a skeptical carpenter has — "what if I pay and it turns out to be useless for my work."

**Where to draw the free tier line:**
- Let people build real, complete designs — full functionality on the design side, no crippling.
- Limit only the expensive-to-you or high-value pieces: a small number of AI-assist uses per month (5–10, tying to the AI usage tiers in the unit economics model), and either a watermarked cutlist or a cap on CNC exports. This lets someone fully experience "this understands my work," while the moment they need it for real production, they hit the wall that leads to paying.
- Don't gate multi-device access (laptop/tablet/phone) — that's a core differentiator that should be felt by everyone, free or not.

**Beyond price — what actually convinces a skeptical tradesperson to switch tools:**
- **Show, don't claim.** Trade audiences distrust marketing language and trust demonstrations. The three demo videos (Section on Launch Content Kit — design flow, AI-assist catch, CNC export) do more convincing than any feature list or ad copy.
- **Let the free tier do the convincing before asking for anything.** Someone trying it themselves, on their own project, at zero risk, is more persuasive than being told it's good.
- **Real testimonials from real workshops, fast.** The Founding Workshop program isn't just a pricing tactic — it's the first source of "a carpenter like me is using this," which matters far more to this audience than any individual feature. Prioritize getting 3–5 genuinely happy early users talking about it in the Facebook groups over anything else in month 1–2.
- **Remove commitment risk on the paid tier too.** No long contract, cancel anytime, month-to-month. Someone who's never used cloud software before is more willing to try €20/month with an easy exit than a signed annual deal.
- **Frame value in their terms, not yours.** Not "AI-assisted design" — "catches a costly mistake before it hits the saw." Not "cloud-based" — "you're not stuck at one computer in the shop." Time and material saved, in their language, beats technical features every time.
- **Address the internet-reliability objection proactively.** A browser-based tool invites one obvious question at a workshop with unreliable internet. Have an honest answer ready — whatever the true offline/caching story is — rather than hoping it doesn't come up.

**Before finalizing the free tier limits:** ask 5–10 people directly, once established as a known, helpful presence in the Facebook groups, what would make them try something like this versus what they currently use. That's faster and more reliable than guessing the right boundary internally.

**Extending the tiers: a Basic non-AI option**

Beyond the free tier, there's a case for a **cheap, AI-free Basic tier at ~$10/month (~€9.50)** — genuinely low-cost to run since it involves no AI API calls, leaving roughly €7/month margin per customer even after Stripe fees, hosting, and tax (a healthier ~75% margin than the blended AI-tier math, because AI cost is the single biggest expense elsewhere in the model). This is a real, sustainable tier — not just a beta discount — for buyers who want the CAD/cutlist/CNC-export value without AI-assist.

**The catch, and the fix:** since AI-assist is the hero feature across all the marketing (tagline, demo videos, ads), a Basic user who never experiences AI once has no way to know they're missing anything — "upgrade if you need more" doesn't work as a mechanism if they can't feel the gap. Worse, someone drawn in by AI-focused marketing who then finds it fully locked away may feel misled, which is a real risk with a trade audience that already distrusts marketing hype.

Fix: **let everyone taste AI once, deliberately, then take it away.** Two ways, either works:
1. **A handful of one-time free AI uses tied to onboarding** — 3–5 free AI-assist uses on the first sample project specifically, not a recurring allowance. Costs almost nothing (a few AI calls per signup), and means nobody buys blind. When those uses run out, "Unlock AI-assist" becomes a concrete, felt need rather than an abstract pitch.
2. **A time-limited full trial instead of a permanent split** — 14–30 days of full Pro access (AI included) before dropping to Basic unless upgraded. Tends to convert better than a capped free tier, since losing something already used is a stronger motivator than imagining something never tried.

For beta specifically, **option 1 is the better starting choice** — simpler to build, cheaper to run, and pairs naturally with the onboarding flow in Section 4a. This also means the PricingTiers structure needs a fourth rung below "Starter": Basic ($10/month, 0 recurring AI requests, one-time onboarding trial only), Starter (€20, 50/month), Pro (€35, 150/month), Studio (€55, 400/month).

## 3c. Pre-Launch Runway (Months -6 to -1)

A 6-month runway before launch is genuinely valuable here — it gives time to build trust *before* asking for anything, which matters enormously in a trade community that's naturally skeptical of outsiders. With only ~4,000+ businesses in the whole addressable market (Section 2), goodwill burned by showing up too promotional too early is costly to earn back. This timeline does presence → value → product → ask in order, instead of compressing it all into a launch week.

**Month -6: Quiet foundation**
- Minimal "coming soon" landing page with just an email waitlist — not the full marketing site yet.
- Create the Facebook Page and YouTube channel (profiles only — no content push yet).
- Join the shortlisted Facebook groups (Section 6) and observe: learn the tone, the recurring pain points, who the respected voices are.
- Start legal/financial setup now (SRL registration, Stripe, e-Factura-compliant invoicing — Section 4b) — tedious to rush later, easy to do calmly with 6 months of runway.
- No product mentions anywhere public yet.

**Month -5: Genuine trust-building**
- Start actually helping in the Facebook groups — answering real questions as a knowledgeable person, zero self-promotion.
- Light educational content on the new channels (general cabinet-design/woodworking tips, not about the app) — one post every week or two.
- Begin identifying a potential anchor customer (Section on client strategies) and build real rapport — no pitch yet.

**Month -4: First soft mentions**
- Start occasionally being open about the project in own posts/comments — "building something for this exact problem" — without a hard pitch.
- Launch the waitlist page publicly, with a referral mechanic ("invite others, move up the list / earn founding pricing") — turns passive signups into a small viral loop before anything is live.
- First behind-the-scenes teaser content — WIP screenshots, "here's a peek."

**Month -3: Private beta recruiting**
- Open a small, limited private beta (15–20 spots) to the warmest waitlist and group contacts.
- Run founder-led concierge onboarding with these testers — live 1:1 sessions building their actual project with them.
- Start collecting real feedback and, with permission, early testimonial quotes.
- **Caveat:** only escalate to this stage once the product can actually handle real hands-on use. A bad first experience with these specific people costs the future advocate group — better to slip a month than rush it.

**Month -2: Proof building**
- Publish real testimonials/snippets from the private beta group — social proof before the public even sees the product.
- Record the polished demo videos, ideally using real private-beta footage rather than staged screens — more credible to a skeptical audience.
- Publish the SEO blog posts now, so they've had time to get indexed by Google before launch traffic arrives.
- Finalize pricing (the annual lock-in Basic tier, Founding Workshop offer, freemium boundaries) based on what the private beta actually revealed.

**Month -1: Countdown**
- Weekly countdown content in the groups and on the page.
- Personally coordinate with the anchor customer to have them visibly active/vocal right at launch.
- Finalize Stripe, invoicing, and legal setup to accept real payments from day one.
- Prep the launch-day founder-story Facebook post and finalize support capacity/FAQ.

**Month 0 — Launch:** the founder-story post goes out, the waitlist converts into the public beta/Founding Workshop offer with real urgency (capped spots), and Phase 1 (Section 5) begins exactly as written.

## 4a. Onboarding & Activation Design

Given the audience — tradespeople who already use professional tools and generally resent forced tutorials — the onboarding approach is **free exploration with ambient guidance**, not a sequential walkthrough.

- **Pre-load a sample cabinet project on first login** instead of a blank canvas. A real, already-built kitchen cabinet a new user can poke at, break, undo, and rebuild is far less intimidating than total freedom on an empty screen, and gives them something to explore *from* rather than toward.
- **Contextual hints instead of sequential steps.** No "Step 1 of 5." Instead, a subtle highlight on the export button the first time someone finishes a design, or a one-line "AI noticed this clearance issue" the first time the assistant actually catches something — surfacing the key differentiators (AI-assist, CNC export) at the moment they'd naturally matter, without gating the path there.
- **Make the differentiators impossible to miss passively.** Since no one is forced toward AI-assist or export, both need to be visually prominent and self-explanatory in the UI regardless of which path a user takes — not buried a few clicks deep.
- **Track activation quietly on the backend, without a forced UI path.** Define activation as something like *first successful cutlist export*, and measure whether people reach it and how. If most free explorers never stumble into it, that's a signal to change the sample project or button placement — not a signal to add a forced tutorial.
- **Collect feedback at a natural pause, not a mid-task popup.** A short one-question email after someone's first session ("What almost stopped you from finishing your first design?") respects their flow better than an in-app modal.
- **Solo-founder support capacity note:** free exploration generates more varied support questions than a guided flow would. Budget for this with a simple FAQ/help panel addressing the 5–10 most likely points of confusion, so the same question isn't answered by hand repeatedly.

## 4b. Legal & Financial Setup (as of Sept 2026 rules)

**Business entity & tax regime:** As of January 1, 2026, Romania's microenterprise regime simplified to a **single 1% tax on revenue** (turnover, not profit) for companies under the €100,000 threshold — the previous 3% sector-based rate was eliminated. For a solo founder bootstrapping from near-zero, this is favorable: light tax burden while reinvesting profit, and an SRL under this regime is the standard setup (a PFA is also possible but makes less sense once scaling toward a team or investors).

**VAT (TVA):** Romania's national VAT registration threshold rose to **RON 395,000** (~€80,000–105,000) from September 2025 — below that, no mandatory domestic VAT registration during the early bootstrap phase. Separately, once expanding to other EU countries, a **pan-EU €10,000 threshold** for digital-services/goods sales triggers VAT obligations in each customer's country, reportable through the single **OSS (One-Stop-Shop)** return rather than registering separately everywhere. For B2B sales to VAT-registered companies elsewhere in the EU, the reverse-charge mechanism usually applies instead (customer self-accounts for VAT, after verifying their VAT number via VIES).

**e-Factura (mandatory e-invoicing):** Plan for this early. The SME enforcement deadline was extended to **July 1, 2026**. From that date, invoices must be transmitted within five working days of issue, with fines that scale by taxpayer size plus a 15% penalty on invoice value for mandatory B2B transactions issued outside the system. **Action: pick invoicing/billing software with native RO e-Factura support from day one** (e.g., SmartBill, FGO, Oblio) rather than retrofitting compliance under deadline pressure later.

**Payments:** Stripe is available to businesses located in Romania, **including sole proprietors**, and its Billing product is built for subscription/recurring-payment models — a direct fit for the freemium + subscription plan. Stripe already holds meaningful share of the subscription-tooling market in Romania. For broader local card coverage, some founders add Netopia or EuPlatesc alongside Stripe, but this isn't necessary to launch.

**AI-assist liability:** Because AI-assist suggestions affect real material and money — a wrong clearance or cutlist could waste expensive material — the ToS needs more than generic GDPR/privacy boilerplate. Worth raising with a Romanian lawyer (this is not legal advice, just what to bring to one):
1. AI-assist output is advisory only, not a guarantee of manufacturability or accuracy — final verification of dimensions, clearances, and cutlists before cutting is the user's responsibility.
2. A standard "as-is" disclaimer with liability capped at fees paid in the prior 12 months (typical SaaS liability cap).
3. Explicit user acknowledgment at signup or first export — not just buried ToS text — creating a clear paper trail.

Pair this with a UX safeguard, not just legal wording: a **"verify before cutting" reminder directly in the app at the export step** protects trust as much as it limits exposure, and matters more to a skeptical trade audience than any clause they'll never read.

**Phase 0 legal/financial checklist:**
1. Register the SRL under the microenterprise 1% regime.
2. Set up Stripe for subscription billing (works immediately, no VAT registration needed at launch volume).
3. Choose e-Factura-compliant invoicing software before it's strictly mandatory for you.
4. Track the OSS €10,000 threshold once selling into other EU countries (Phase 3) — that's the trigger point, not overall company revenue.
5. GDPR-compliant privacy policy and ToS (per Section 4, item 5) — still a Phase 0 prerequisite.
6. Draft the AI-assist liability clause with a Romanian lawyer alongside the ToS — same conversation, same prerequisite.

## 5. Phase 1 — Romania Beta Launch (Months 1–3)

**Goal:** 100–300 signed-up users, 10–30 paying "Founding Workshops," a working feedback loop, and 2–3 case studies. Spend: minimal, mostly time.

### 5.1 Organic / Community Channels (primary engine — this is where the budget-free growth has to come from)

- **Facebook groups for tâmplari/mobilă la comandă in Romania**: Join the 5–10 most active groups (search "tâmplari," "mobilă la comandă," "CNC lemn," "proiectare mobilă"). Don't spam — contribute genuinely (answer questions, share useful tips) for 2–3 weeks before ever mentioning the app. Then share it as "I built this because I got tired of X" — founder-story posts consistently outperform ad-style posts in trade communities.
- **YouTube demo/tutorial content**: 5–10 short videos ("How to design a corner kitchen cabinet in 10 minutes," "Cutlist export walkthrough," "AI-assisted layout demo"). This is searchable, evergreen, and doubles as your onboarding material later.
- **Local associations**: Reach out to Romanian furniture/wood industry associations (e.g., APMR — Asociația Producătorilor de Mobilă din România — or regional wood-processing clusters) — even a mention in a newsletter or a webinar invite is free reach into your exact audience.
- **Trade shows** (even without a budget for a booth): BIFE-SIM (Bucharest furniture/wood industry fair) and regional wood-industry expos. Attending as a visitor, talking to 20–30 workshop owners directly, and demoing on your tablet costs only your time and travel — extremely high-signal for a B2B niche product.
- **SEO content in Romanian**: Blog posts targeting long-tail search terms your buyers actually type — "program proiectare mobilă gratuit," "cum fac cutlist pentru CNC," "alternativă Polyboard." Low competition in Romanian for these terms means even a few well-written posts can rank.

### 5.2 Low-cost paid (only once first profit exists — this is your reinvestment starting point)

- **Facebook/Instagram ads geo-targeted to Romania**, interest-targeted to woodworking/carpentry/CNC pages — even €5–10/day is enough to test messaging before scaling. Best used to retarget people who visited the website but didn't sign up.
- **Google Ads** on long-tail Romanian keywords (cheap CPCs in this niche/market) — again, small daily budget, tightly targeted.

### 5.3 Referral loop (built once, works forever)

- Every "Founding Workshop" user gets a simple referral incentive (e.g., one free month for both sides) for inviting another workshop. In a trade where everyone knows everyone locally, this is disproportionately effective and costs nothing but a feature to build.

### 5.4 Beta Client-Acquisition Strategies

Five concrete ways to get real clients even while still in beta, ranked roughly by how much they compound versus how much founder time they cost:

1. **Annual prepay lock-in.** Offer the first ~50 beta users 12 months of Basic access for $10/month, paid upfront as $120/year rather than billed monthly. The upfront-annual structure matters more than the price itself: 10 people prepaying $120 puts $1,200 in the account in week one — enough to jump straight to a real ad budget test (Section 5.2) instead of waiting months for the reinvestment loop (Section 6) to build up on its own. Cap this at a firm number (50 users) rather than leaving it open-ended, since the margin on this tier is thin by design (see Section 3b's Basic tier math).
2. **Founder-led concierge onboarding.** At zero users, trust is the constraint, not volume. Personally offer to build a beta user's actual cabinet project with them live, over video call or in person, the first time they use it. This doubles as a sales demo and a source of real product feedback. Doesn't scale past ~20–30 people, but that's exactly the size needed at beta stage.
3. **Referral chain within the trade circle.** Beyond the general referral loop in 5.3, give each beta user a concrete reason to bring in one more carpenter specifically — an extra free month, or an AI-request top-up — rather than hoping word-of-mouth happens on its own.
4. **Frame it as "Founding Partner," not "beta tester."** "Beta" signals unfinished and risky to a cautious buyer. Reframe the same offer as co-creation: "help shape the tool Romanian workshops actually need, get locked-in pricing for life as thanks." Visible recognition — a name credit, a private feedback group, first access to new features — makes people meaningfully less price-sensitive than treating them as customers buying unfinished software.
5. **One well-connected anchor customer.** Identify one respected, well-networked workshop owner in the Facebook groups — someone other carpenters already ask for advice — and give them extra attention (free premium tier, direct support access, co-developing a feature they specifically want). One vocal advocate with real standing in the community is worth more than ten anonymous free signups, because their endorsement carries weight the group already trusts. This is the same anchor customer referenced in the pre-launch runway (Section 3c, month -5).

## 6. The Reinvestment Loop (how "start with tier 1 budget" actually scales)

Since the model is "reinvest all profit," structure it explicitly so it doesn't stall:

1. **Free tier** → signups (cost: $0, just server load)
2. **Paid conversions** (Founding Workshop pricing) → first cash
3. **100% of net profit** goes into: (a) small paid ad tests, (b) content production (better demo videos, maybe a freelance video editor for a few hours), (c) attending one trade show
4. **Track CAC and payback period from day one** — even informally — so you know which channel to pour the next euro of profit into. Without this, "reinvest everything" just means "spend randomly."
5. Revisit budget tier monthly: as MRR grows, graduate from "under €500/month" toward the next bracket deliberately, not accidentally.

## 6a. Retention & Post-Purchase Playbook

The plan is acquisition-heavy through Phase 3 — this section balances it, because retention is a bigger lever than it looks. In the base-case unit economics model, dropping monthly churn from 5% to 3% stretches average customer lifetime from 20 to 33 months and pushes LTV from ~€283 to ~€467 — a bigger swing than most single acquisition tactics could produce.

**Concrete tactics:**
- **Monthly changelog to paying users** — keeps engagement, reduces silent churn from feeling forgotten.
- **Renewal reminders for the annual lock-in tier** — 30 days and 7 days before renewal, with cancellation made easy but requiring explicit action (avoids the resentment of surprise auto-renewal while still respecting that cancellation shouldn't be a maze).
- **Quarterly personal check-in** with Founding Workshop/Founding Partner members — feasible manually at small scale, doubles as feedback and relationship-building, and extends the founder concierge approach from Section 5.4.
- **Proactive outreach on inactivity** — if a paying user hasn't logged in for 14+ days, a personal "need help with anything?" message before they silently cancel.
- **Win-back flow** — an automatic email ~30 days after cancellation with an incentive to return.
- **Visible feature-request channel with "you asked, we built" follow-ups** — creates a feeling of ownership that's stickier than any single feature.
- **A private paying-customer community** (a smaller Facebook or WhatsApp group, separate from the public trade groups) — peer support reduces reliance on the founder for every question and builds a switching cost through community, not just software.

**Metrics refinement:** split churn in Section 9's tracking into **voluntary cancellation vs. failed-payment churn**. Many "cancellations" are just an expired card, not dissatisfaction — Stripe's automatic retry/dunning handles most of this without any retention effort at all, so don't over-diagnose failed-payment churn as a product problem.

## 7. Phase 2 — Romania Growth + Segment Expansion (Months 4–9)

Once there's a base of workshop users, revenue, and testimonials:

- **Bring in designers/architects**: They care about client presentation quality and speed of iteration — position cloud collaboration ("share a live design link with your client") as the hook. Reach them via interior design Facebook/LinkedIn groups and local design-school partnerships (guest webinar, student discount).
- **Bring in manufacturers**: Longer sales cycle — this is where a few direct outreach conversations (LinkedIn, trade show follow-ups) matter more than ads. Use your carpenter case studies as proof before pitching larger shops.
- **Build the first real content/social cadence** — by now there's revenue to justify a few hours/week of paid help (freelance social media management, video editing) instead of doing all of it solo.
- **Consider hardware/software partnerships**: Fittings distributors (Blum, Hettich, GTV) and CNC machine sellers in Romania have existing relationships with your exact audience. A co-marketing or integration conversation (e.g., "hardware presets" in your app) is a natural, low-cost distribution partnership once you have traction to show them.

## 8. Phase 3 — European Expansion (Month 10+)

Do **not** expand country-by-country with a copy-pasted Romanian playbook. Each market needs:

- **Localization**: Full UI/content translation (not just Romanian/English — German, Polish, French, Italian as priorities, since these have large woodworking/joinery trade populations), and GDPR compliance is already in place from Phase 0.
- **Competitive landscape check per country**: Polyboard is strong in France; CabinetVision/SketchList lean Anglo markets. Know the entrenched player in each target country before messaging.
- **Same beachhead logic, per country**: independent workshops first, then designers/manufacturers, using the same organic-community-first approach (local trade Facebook/LinkedIn groups, local trade shows, local SEO).
- **Sequencing suggestion**: Poland and Hungary next (proximity, similar workshop-driven furniture trade structure, lower ad costs), then Western Europe (France, Germany, Italy) once pricing and case studies can support the higher expectations and competition there.

## 11. Risks to Watch (Solo-Founder Specific)

- **Support load**: A web-based CAD tool for a technical trade will generate support questions. Budget your own time for this in Phase 1 — it doubles as product feedback, but don't let it silently eat all your marketing time.
- **Message dilution**: Resist the pull to advertise to all three segments simultaneously before you have proof points. Sequential beats simultaneous here.
- **Free tier cannibalization**: Watch that the free tier doesn't satisfy workshop owners fully — if nobody upgrades, the boundary needs tightening (usually: limit exports/cutlist detail, not core design features).
- **Founder burnout**: Bootstrapped, solo, reinvest-everything models are slow by design. Set realistic month 1–3 expectations (dozens of users, not thousands) so the pace doesn't feel like failure. See the suggested weekly time budget below for a concrete way to avoid this rather than just naming it.

**Competitive response — what if PolyBoard or CabinetVision reacts?**

They could plausibly launch a cheap cloud tier, bolt on "AI" features, or undercut price temporarily. But both carry real structural drag that slows this down: they're built on legacy desktop architecture, so a genuine cloud rewrite is years, not months — and their existing customers already paid large one-time perpetual licenses, so a cheap subscription risks cannibalizing their own base and alienating their reseller channel. That friction is the actual first-mover window here, not a false sense of safety.

The defense isn't feature-matching — features are copyable; relationships and local trust aren't, at least not quickly. The Romanian-first origin story, personal founder relationships with early customers (Section 5.4), and the private paying-customer community (Section 6a) all compound into a moat a foreign incumbent can't replicate fast. Practically:
- A quarterly (not obsessive) calendar reminder to check competitor pricing pages and announcements.
- Use the first-mover window to lock in Founding Workshop/Founding Partner customers on 12-month pricing while it's open.
- If a competitor drops price aggressively, don't chase it indefinitely — the unit economics don't support unlimited discounting (Section on unit economics). Double down on responsiveness and local support instead, which is the actual defensible advantage.

**Suggested weekly founder time budget**

- **Pre-launch (months -6 to -2):** ~10–12 hrs/week — community engagement (2–3 hrs), content creation (3–4 hrs), legal/admin setup (front-loaded early, per Section 3c).
- **Launch month:** ~20 hrs/week temporarily — concierge onboarding calls (Section 5.4) and countdown content (Section 3c, month -1) both spike at once.
- **Steady-state post-launch:** ~8–10 hrs/week — roughly 2 hrs community, 2 hrs content, 2 hrs support, 2 hrs metrics/admin.
- **If time runs short, cut in this order:** blog content first, then social posting cadence. Never cut customer support or Facebook group presence first — both are trust-critical in a market this small (Section 2), and losing trust here is far more costly than a missed blog post.
